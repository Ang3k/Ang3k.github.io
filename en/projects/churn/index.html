Machine Learning for Prediction of Credit Card Churn

01 Introduction to the Dataset 02 Exploratory Data Analysis 03 Data Preparation 04 Machine Learning Predictions 05 Insights and Conclusions

PART 1

Introduction to the Dataset

1 - Business Context

A bank manager is worried about the growing number of customers leaving the credit card service. The bank would greatly appreciate being able to predict which customers are going to cancel, so it can act proactively, offering better service and changing those customers' minds.

The manager found a possible solution by hiring Angel, a data scientist, to extract insights from a dataset through exploratory data analysis. He also asked Angel to build a Machine Learning model that could predict, based on the data, whether or not a customer would cancel their credit card, and to report the model's accuracy.

2 - Data Dictionary

  • CLIENTNUM: Unique identification number of the customer who holds the account.
  • Attrition_Flag: Internal event variable (customer activity)
  • Customer_Age: Demographic variable - Customer's age in years.
  • Gender: Demographic variable - Customer's gender
  • Dependent_count: Demographic variable - Number of dependents.
  • Education_Level: Demographic variable - Education level of the account holder
  • Marital_Status: Demographic variable - Marital status
  • Income_Category: Demographic variable - Annual income category of the account holder
  • Card_Category: Product variable - Card type (Blue, Silver, Gold, Platinum).
  • Months_on_book: Length of the relationship with the bank (in months).
  • Months_on_book: Length of the relationship with the bank (in months).
  • Months_Inactive_12_mon: Number of inactive months in the last 12 months.
  • Contacts_Count_12_mon: Number of contacts in the last 12 months.
  • Credit_Limit: Credit limit on the credit card.
  • Total_Revolving_Bal: Total revolving balance on the credit card.
  • Avg_Open_To_Buy: Available credit line (average of the last 12 months).
  • Total_Amt_Chng_Q4_Q1: Change in transaction amount
  • Total_Trans_Amt: Total transaction amount (last 12 months).
  • Total_Trans_Ct: Total number of transactions (last 12 months).
  • Total_Ct_Chng_Q4_Q1: Change in number of transactions
  • Avg_Utilization_Ratio: Average card utilization ratio.
  • Total_Relationship_Count: Total number of products held by the customer.

In this project, I chose to follow a data problem-solving methodology presented in one of the most renowned books on Machine Learning, "Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn & TensorFlow" by Aurélien Géron. Below are the recommended steps I follow throughout this project:

  • Look at the big picture.

  • Prepare the data for Machine Learning algorithms.
  • Select a model and train it.

4 - Project Solution Requirements

In the conversation with the manager, Angel asked what the main information he wanted to obtain was. In response, the manager defined a set of strategic business questions, highlighting the ones that would be essential to guide the analyses and decisions. Here are the questions to be answered:

  • What is the profile of the customers most likely to cancel their card?
  • How does customer gender affect financial condition and cancellation rates?
  • Does education level influence the likelihood of canceling the card?
  • Do customers who use their card more receive higher or lower credit limits?
  • Are customers with high incomes more likely to cancel their cards?
  • What behaviors are common among customers who cancel their cards?

Besides these questions, the manager asked Angel to train a Machine Learning model with accuracy above 85%. He stressed the importance of choosing an easily interpretable model, since that would let the bank better understand how the algorithm works and have more control over its decisions.

  • Build a Machine Learning model with accuracy above 85%.
  • Identify the main characteristics that influence customer churn.
  • Make sure the model is easily interpretable (optional)

PART 3

Data Preparation

1 - Strategies and Procedures

Just like cleaning a bedroom or a living room, cleaning a dataset requires a procedure: you can't start by sweeping and then throw the water on the floor! That is why, in this and other projects, I usually follow this order: removing useless columns, identifying and removing null values, identifying and removing outliers, creating dummy variables and encoding. This ensures a clean dataset, ready to work in an ML model.

2 - Handling Null Values

As I worked with the dataset, I noticed there were no "np.nan" values, the null values we usually work with, because they appeared in another form, as "Unknown". So I created a function to convert these into "np.nan", making the work easier. I then created two more functions: one that returned a dataframe with the sum of null values for each column, and another that generated a bar chart showing the percentage of missing values for each feature, that is, each column.

Looking at this chart, I saw that only a few rows had missing values. However, since the dataset has few rows, I did not want to drop any row with "np.nan". Instead, I chose to fill them with the value of the next cell, and to make this more accurate, I first sorted by "Credit_Limit", so neighboring cells would theoretically be closer to each other, since this column had already proven to be very impactful.

3 - Handling Outliers

To identify and remove outliers, I used John Tukey's well-known method, the same one used to build a boxplot. I created a function that returns a dataframe filtered between a lower and an upper bound, defined from the percentiles of the column being processed, selected the columns that could have outliers and applied the method to each of them.

4 - Encoding Categorical Variables

As a last step, I needed to encode the categorical variables as numbers. Since the dataset had relatively few rows, I tried to use "pd.get_dummies" as little as possible, because it creates extra columns for each category. So I only turned "Gender" and "Relationship_Status" into dummies, creating 3 new columns, since "drop_first = True".

For the other categorical variables, there was an implicit order of importance; for example, a "Platinum" card is expected to rank above a "Gold" card. I defined this order in several lists and used the sklearn.preprocessing function to assign numerical values that reflect this natural order. With the dataset completely clean, we move on to building the ML models.

Then I picked some of the main classic Machine Learning models to test their performance, such as DecisionTreeClassifier, RandomForest, XGBoost and others shown in the code. Regardless of the model selected, the results are positive, with accuracy above 87% for every model. However, these models were created with default hyperparameters, which are probably not the best for maximizing accuracy. So I decided to tune 3 of them: XGBoost, because it is the most accurate model, and DecisionTreeClassifier and RandomForestClassifier, because their nature gives a clearer view of how the algorithm decides the "y" of a given row, especially the DecisionTreeClassifier. The code then builds a dictionary to organize and compare these predictions with the actual values, which are the true labels of the test set. 4 - Feature Importance Analysis

Using the feature_importances_ attribute, we can determine how important each column of the training dataset is, making it easier to identify the most relevant factors to consider. This analysis can better guide decision-making by the bank's staff. In the code, three horizontal bar plots are generated, using a combination of enumerate and zip to get the index and iterate over both lists at the same time.

These plots are interesting because they show how each model has its own way of understanding the information. Still, there are some clear patterns, such as columns like "Average Open to Buy" and "Income Lvl" always staying at the top, which indicates they are extremely relevant. On the other hand, others are consistently at the bottom, such as "Education", "Dependents" and "Gender", which shows these columns are almost dispensable for predicting customer churn and would probably not change training if removed.

4 - Confusion Matrix Analysis

The confusion matrix is essential for evaluating classification models, since it gives a detailed view of true positives, false positives, true negatives and false negatives. This helps identify where the model makes mistakes and how to improve its accuracy. This code plots the confusion matrices for three classification models: XGBoost, Decision Tree and Random Forest. It calculates each matrix from the models' predictions and the true labels, shows them in subplots and displays each figure with the chosen palette and a custom title with the model name.

The matrices show similar results, as expected given the models' accuracy: the DecisionTreeClassifier has the most false positives and negatives, and XGBoost the fewest. This highlights the importance of small percentages mentioned above: even though XGBoost has only a few percentage points of accuracy advantage, it avoids false positives/negatives up to 2.5x better than the others. Assuming, as a low estimate, that a bank customer generates $100 in profit, this could mean up to $25,000 in extra profit.

5 - Visualizing the Decision Trees

The code visualizes the decision trees of three models: it uses Scikit-learn's "plot_tree" for the DTC and for a random estimator from the RFC, while XGBoost is visualized with its own function. The figure is set up with three subplots, and the charts show the tree structure up to three levels deep, with readable feature names. The full charts are available on GitHub.

6 - Insights and Conclusions

Finally, after a detailed analysis, we gathered a wide range of valuable insights. They are more than enough to give complete, well-founded answers to the questions raised at the start of the project, and here are the answers:

What is the profile of the customers most likely to cancel their card?

Older customers who make fewer transactions and have credit limits similar to active customers are at higher risk of canceling.

How does customer gender affect financial condition and cancellation rates?

Women tend to have lower credit limits but use their cards more often. This leads to different usage patterns between genders.

Does education level influence the likelihood of canceling the card?

People with higher education levels tend to be more likely to cancel their cards

Do customers who use their card more receive higher or lower credit limits?

Customers who make more transactions often receive higher credit limits, since total transaction amount is directly related to the limit.

Are customers with high incomes more likely to cancel their cards?

Customers with higher incomes show a greater tendency to cancel.

What behaviors are common among customers who cancel their cards?

Customers who cancel tend to make fewer transactions and use their card less actively than those who stay with the service.

We also met the requirements for the Machine Learning models, building three models with accuracy above 85%. Each model comes with a detailed analysis of feature importance, which clearly and effectively identifies the main factors driving the predictions.

In addition, the DecisionTreeClassifier meets the high interpretability requirement, standing out for its simple structure, which allows easy visualization and clear, direct decision rules using a single tree. This is unlike more complex models such as RandomForestClassifier and XGBoost, which use multiple decision trees (Random Forest) or a set of trees with adaptive learning (XGBoost).
